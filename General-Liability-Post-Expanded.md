## How Much General Liability Insurance Do You Really Need? An Honest Breakdown for NC Small Business Owners

If you're shopping for general liability insurance for a small business, you've probably noticed every carrier asks the same first question: "How much coverage do you need?" And every insurance website seems to give you the same vague answer: "It depends." That isn't helpful — and it isn't honest.

The honest answer is: most small businesses can pick the right general liability limits in about ten minutes if they understand three numbers, two scenarios, and one rule of thumb. This article walks you through all of it, with a comparison table, a decision tree, and frequently asked questions about general liability coverage limits.

### The Three Numbers on a General Liability Policy

Every general liability policy quotes the same three numbers, almost always in this order:

1. **Per-occurrence limit** — the maximum the policy pays for any single covered incident
2. **Aggregate limit** — the maximum the policy pays across ALL covered incidents during the policy year
3. **Products-completed operations aggregate** — a separate aggregate just for claims arising from products you sold or work you completed

When you see a quote like "$1,000,000 per occurrence / $2,000,000 aggregate," that's the first two numbers. The standard general liability limits for most small businesses look like one of these three combinations:

| Coverage tier | Per occurrence | Aggregate | Typical business size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry-level | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 | Sole proprietors, freelancers, micro-businesses |
| **Standard** | **$1,000,000** | **$2,000,000** | **Most small businesses (1–25 employees)** |
| Enhanced | $2,000,000 | $4,000,000 | Mid-sized businesses, contract requirements |
| High-limit | $5,000,000 | $5,000,000 | Specialty industries, larger operations |

The middle row — $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate — is what most quotes call "standard general liability limits." It's the default for a reason: it handles the vast majority of small business claims, it costs surprisingly little (often $400 to $1,200 per year for a low-risk business), and it's the limit most landlords, clients, and commercial contracts require.

### Why the "$1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate" Standard Exists

A $1 million per-occurrence limit isn't an arbitrary number. Insurance carriers and risk managers settled on it for three reasons:

**First**, the average general liability claim involving bodily injury settles for well under $1 million. Slip-and-fall claims, property damage incidents, and minor injury suits typically resolve in the $20,000 to $200,000 range. A $1 million limit gives you 5–10x headroom over the typical claim — enough to handle outliers comfortably.

**Second**, $1 million is the floor that most third parties require. Commercial landlords, construction GCs hiring subcontractors, vendors at trade shows, and clients hiring service providers virtually all require their counterparties to carry at least $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate. If your limits are lower, you cannot do business with them.

**Third**, the gap from $1 million to $2 million in coverage costs surprisingly little additional premium — often $100 to $300 per year for a low-risk business. Most small business owners decide the extra coverage is worth it.

### When $1M/$2M Is Plenty

For these businesses, standard $1M/$2M general liability limits are typically sufficient:

- **Professional services with no physical office**: consultants, freelance designers, virtual assistants, online coaches. Your liability exposure is largely contractual (handled by professional liability) rather than premises-based.
- **Home-based businesses with minimal customer interaction**: handmade goods sellers, dropshippers, online retailers without warehouses. The "general public visiting your premises" risk that drives most GL claims is minimal.
- **Small retail with low foot traffic**: a one-person specialty shop in a small downtown space. Slip-and-fall risk exists but is bounded by your floor space and customer volume.
- **Low-hazard service businesses**: pet groomers, tutors, lawn-mowing operators with no employees. Standard limits cover the vast majority of realistic claim scenarios.

### When You Should Consider Higher Limits

Higher limits — $2M per occurrence, $4M+ aggregate, or specialty $5M coverage — make sense in these cases:

- **You sign contracts that require it.** Construction GCs commonly require subcontractors to carry $2M per occurrence. Federal contracts often require $5M or higher. Commercial landlords for high-end retail or restaurant space sometimes require higher limits. Look at every contract you sign — if it specifies coverage limits, those limits become your floor.
- **You have foreseeable exposure beyond $1M.** A bounce-house rental company at a 200-person event, a contractor working on $5M custom homes, a restaurant serving alcohol to crowds of 100+, or a fitness studio with strenuous classes all have realistic claim scenarios that could blow past $1 million.
- **You're growing past a million in revenue.** Once your business has meaningful assets — cash, equipment, real estate, retained earnings — those assets become the target in a lawsuit. Higher limits protect what you've built.
- **You're in a high-litigation industry.** Restaurants, bars, fitness centers, daycare facilities, and contractors are sued more often than the typical small business. Higher limits are appropriate insurance against this elevated frequency, not just severity.

### The Decision Tree

Here's how I'd think about it as a small business owner:

```
Do you have any contracts that require specific limits?
  ├─ Yes → Carry at least the required limit. Add 25–50% as a buffer.
  └─ No → Continue.

Is your business in a higher-risk category?
  (Restaurants, bars, contractors, fitness, daycare, events, anything serving alcohol)
  ├─ Yes → Start with $2M per occurrence / $4M aggregate.
  └─ No → Continue.

Do you have annual revenue above $500k OR substantial assets to protect?
  ├─ Yes → $2M per occurrence / $4M aggregate is appropriate.
  └─ No → $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate is the standard floor.
```

For most NC small businesses we work with at BlackArrow, this decision tree resolves to $1M/$2M as the floor and $2M/$4M as the appropriate upgrade. Going to $5M aggregate is generally driven by specific contract requirements rather than risk math.

### What About $1M/$3M and $2M/$2M Combinations?

You'll occasionally see oddball quote combinations like $1M per occurrence with a $3M aggregate, or $2M per occurrence with a $2M aggregate. These exist for specific industries and contract requirements. The general rule is:

- **The aggregate should be at least 2x the per-occurrence limit** so you can handle multiple claims in a year.
- **The aggregate equal to the per-occurrence limit is a warning sign**. If you have multiple claims, the policy can be exhausted by a single bad incident, leaving you uninsured for the rest of the year.

If your quote shows a $2M/$2M structure, ask your agent whether $2M/$4M is available — the premium difference is usually small and the protection difference is meaningful.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**How much general liability insurance do I need for a small business?**

Most small businesses should start with $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate as standard general liability limits. This handles the vast majority of claim scenarios, satisfies most third-party contract requirements, and costs $400–$2,500 annually for low- to moderate-risk businesses. If you sign contracts requiring higher limits, work in a high-litigation industry, or have substantial assets to protect, consider $2M per occurrence / $4M aggregate.

**What does "$1 million per occurrence / $2 million aggregate" actually mean?**

The per-occurrence limit ($1M) is the maximum the policy pays for any single covered incident. The aggregate limit ($2M) is the maximum the policy pays across all incidents during the policy year. So if a single slip-and-fall claim settles for $800,000, the per-occurrence limit covers it, and your remaining aggregate becomes $1.2M for any other claims that year. If a single claim exceeds $1M, you're personally on the hook for the difference.

**Is $1 million general liability enough for an LLC?**

For most small LLCs, $1 million per occurrence is sufficient. The "LLC" part is irrelevant to insurance limits — the structure protects your personal assets from business liabilities only to the extent your business has assets and proper liability insurance. If the LLC is properly maintained and you carry adequate GL coverage, $1M is enough for most operations. LLCs with higher revenue, employees, or specific high-risk operations should consider $2M.

**What is a $5 million aggregate insurance policy?**

A $5 million aggregate policy pays up to $5 million across all covered claims during the policy year. It's typically structured as either $5M/$5M (per-occurrence equal to aggregate) or $2M/$5M (per-occurrence lower than aggregate). $5M policies are common for federal contractors, large construction subs, specialty businesses, and any business required to carry higher limits by contract.

**Why is the aggregate higher than the per-occurrence limit?**

The aggregate is set higher than the per-occurrence limit so the policy can handle multiple claims in a year without being exhausted. A $1M/$2M structure means a single major claim ($800k slip-and-fall) doesn't deplete your coverage for the remaining 11 months of the policy year. If the aggregate equals the per-occurrence limit, one bad claim can leave you uninsured for the rest of the year.

**Do I need general liability if my business is home-based?**

If you have any customer contact (even via shipped products), any business-related activity outside your home, or any contractual relationships, general liability is strongly recommended. Home-based businesses sometimes assume their homeowners insurance covers business activities — it does not. Standard homeowners policies explicitly exclude business activities, leaving you exposed for product liability, customer injury, professional negligence, and contract breach claims.

**Does general liability cover my employees if they're injured?**

No. General liability covers third parties — customers, vendors, the public. Employee injuries are covered by workers' compensation insurance, which is a separate policy required by NC law for most employers with three or more employees.

**Does general liability cover professional negligence or errors?**

No. General liability covers bodily injury and property damage to third parties — slip-and-falls, customer injuries, damaged property. Errors and omissions in your professional services (a consultant giving bad advice, a designer missing a deadline, a contractor's faulty workmanship) are covered by professional liability insurance, sometimes called errors & omissions (E&O) insurance.

**What's the difference between general liability and a Business Owner's Policy (BOP)?**

A Business Owner's Policy bundles general liability with commercial property insurance (and sometimes business interruption coverage) in a single policy at a discount. For most small businesses with a physical location, a BOP delivers the same general liability coverage you'd get standalone, plus property coverage, for less than the two policies would cost separately. We typically quote both options so you can compare.

**How can I lower my general liability insurance cost?**

Five proven ways to reduce GL premiums: (1) Bundle into a BOP if you have commercial property exposure. (2) Increase your deductible — most policies allow $250 to $2,500 deductible options. (3) Document a safety program for your industry. (4) Avoid claims — your loss history affects pricing for years. (5) Shop carriers. Working with an independent agent who compares quotes across 20+ carriers (like BlackArrow) typically saves 15–30% versus a single-carrier agent.

### The Bottom Line

For most NC small businesses, $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate is the right starting point for general liability insurance. Step up to $2M/$4M if you're in a higher-risk industry, have contracts requiring higher limits, or have substantial assets to protect. Avoid the $1M/$1M trap where a single bad claim can leave you uninsured for the rest of the year.
